                                                                                 Abstract
                                                 The adaptive clinching auction of Dobzinski, Lavi, and Nisan (FOCS’08) is
                                             a cornerstone of mechanism design with budget-constrained bidders, and it has
                                             since inspired a broad range of extensions to substantially richer market set-
                                             tings. Adaptive clinching enjoys compelling guarantees: it is Pareto optimal,
                                             and truthful bidding is a dominant strategy whenever budgets cannot be over-
                                             reported. In many real-world environments, however, identities are cheap to
                                             create, making resilience to Sybil attacks an additional requirement. Whether
                                             the guarantees of adaptive clinching survive when a bidder can split its budget
                                             across fake identities has remained open. We introduce a generalized framework
                                             for adaptive clinching and prove that it satisfies Sybil-robust incentive compati-
                                             bility: a bidder cannot benefit from splitting into any finite number of identities
                                             with arbitrary value reports, provided their aggregate reported budget does not
                                             exceed the bidder’s enforceable true budget. Sybil resilience thus joins the list
                                             of ideal properties of adaptive clinching.
                                                 Our work is motivated by practical mechanism-design problems in blockchain-
                                             based decentralized finance, where two-asset automated market makers (AMMs)
                                             are among the most widely used applications. We design a new AMM adaptive
                                             clinching mechanism that extends classical adaptive clinching in two ways: it
                                             supports a two-sided market and protects against two-sided strategies, and it
                                             allows the supply function to depend on the auction state. Its one-sided special
                                             case is captured by our generalized adaptive clinching framework, and we further
                                             extend our proof techniques to show that the full two-sided mechanism simul-
                                             taneously satisfies Sybil-robust incentive compatibility and a relaxed notion of
                                             Pareto optimality. In light of known impossibility results, together with a new
                                             impossibility proved in this paper, we argue that these guarantees are essentially
                                             the best possible. More broadly, our work reveals new connections between clas-
                                             sical algorithmic game theory and modern decentralized blockchains.

1     Introduction
Automated Market Makers (AMMs) are arguably the most widely used application in
decentralized finance (DeFi). An AMM is a smart contract that stands ready to trade
with anyone: rather than matching buyers against sellers through an order book, it
holds an inventory of assets and quotes an exchange rate determined by a publicly
known pricing curve. By far the most common deployment is the two-asset AMM,
exemplified by Uniswap [Uni].
    In a typical two-asset AMM, users trade one of two assets, denoted X and Y ,
against a smart contract, which we henceforth also refer to as the pool. Initially, the
state of the pool is denoted by (x0 , y0 ), meaning that the pool holds x0 units of X and
y0 units of Y . The exchange rate for each trade is determined by a predefined pricing
curve. For example, the widely adopted constant-product rule requires

                                 (x0 − x)(y0 + y) = C

for some positive constant C. Thus, starting from state (x0 , y0 ), a user who contributes
y units of Y to the pool receives x units of X. Following the trade, the pool transitions
to state (x0 −x, y0 +y), preserving the invariant that the product of the reserves remains
equal to C.

Widespread incentive attacks. Because the price a user receives depends on the
pool state when its trade executes, and that state is moved by every preceding trade
in the same block, an AMM is acutely sensitive to who controls the contents and the
ordering of a block. A block builder can exploit this directly. Suppose a user submits
an order to buy X with Y . Immediately before that order, the builder buys X cheaply
for itself, driving the price up to the user’s declared slippage tolerance—the worst price
at which it is still willing to trade. The user’s order then executes at that price and
pushes the price higher still, and the builder sells immediately after it. The builder has
thus bought low and sold high within a single block, at essentially no risk, since it sees
the user’s order before committing to either leg. The two legs are known respectively
as front-running and back-running, and the pair as a sandwich attack; a substantial
body of work [ZQT+ 21, Par23, KDC22] documents how prevalent and how profitable
such attacks are in practice.
    Such incentive attacks are commonly referred to as Miner (or Maximum) Ex-
tractable Value (MEV), and the harm they do extends well beyond the users who are
sandwiched. Because the profit accrues to whoever controls the contents and ordering
of a block, MEV fosters opaque off-chain markets in which block builders, traders,
and specialized searchers compete to identify and exploit these opportunities, and it
rewards most those participants with the best access to order flow and the deepest
capital. These forces push the ecosystem steadily toward centralization. Indeed, a re-
cent measurement study by Yang et al. [YNZ25] found that the top two block builders
produce more than 85% of Ethereum blocks, severely undermining the decentralized
vision of blockchain systems.

A theory on AMM mechanism design. Motivated by these attacks, a line of
work [CWS25, LSZ26, SZCL26] has formulated AMM as a mechanism-design problem.
In this formulation, the AMM mechanism receives a batch of bids (typically all trade

                                            3
requests contained in one block). It then decides, for every bid, how much of each
asset that bid sends and receives. Each bid comes in one of two directions. A sell-Y
bid specifies a value v and a budget Q, meaning that it wants to buy X using at most
Q units of Y , and that it values each unit of X it acquires at v units of Y . A sell-X
bid is defined symmetrically.
    Two structural features distinguish the problem from traditional auctions. First,
the market is two-sided: a batch may contain both sell-X and sell-Y bids, which can
trade with each other as well as with the pool. Second, the mechanism must exactly
settle: every token paid out is funded either by an opposite-side bid or by the pool,
no outside subsidy is used, no token is discarded, and the end state of the pool lands
back on the prescribed pricing curve.
    Within this formulation, one may ask for a dream AMM mechanism, namely one
that simultaneously achieves the following two desiderata.
1. Sybil-robust incentive compatibility. The mechanism should encourage truth-
   ful bidding by an individual trader, and it should moreover be strategy-proof
   against a block builder who may itself be a trader with intrinsic value and demand.
   Concretely, a trader or builder can misreport both its value and its budget, and
   can inject any finite number of fake bids under pseudonymous identities, carrying
   arbitrary values and budgets. Further, a builder can arbitrarily sequence the bids
   contained in the batch. We refer to the resulting incentive notion as Sybil-robust
   incentive compatibility. We stress that Sybil-robustness is critical in blockchain
   environments since creating arbitrary pseudonyms is essentially free.
2. Pareto optimality. The outcome should be Pareto optimal: no incremental trade
   among a subset of traders can make some of them better off without making any of
   them worse off, and no trader can improve its own utility by trading further with
   the pool.
    Throughout the paper, we assume that a strategic builder cannot censor other
traders’ bids, meaning that every submitted bid must be included in the mechanism’s
input. This matches the usual assumption in classical mechanism design: a strategic
participant may manipulate its own reports, or even inject fake bids (Sybil attacks),
but cannot suppress the reports submitted by other participants. In practice, this
is ensured in blockchain systems when the underlying consensus protocol provides
censorship resilience [AER25, foc, GNR25], a guarantee that major blockchain projects
such as Ethereum are committed to offering at the consensus layer [foc]. Such an
assumption is also unavoidable: it is known that once censorship is admissible, Sybil-
robust incentive compatibility is unattainable in AMM mechanism design even without
imposing any efficiency requirement [SZCL26].

Impossibility of a dream AMM mechanism. Unfortunately, such a dream AMM
mechanism is unattainable. Shi et al. [SZCL26] and Li et al. [LSZ26] show that
Pareto optimality and Sybil-robust incentive compatibility are mutually incompati-
ble for exact-settlement AMM mechanisms. Further, this impossibility holds in a very
strong sense, even when the strategy space consists only of single-identity valuation
misreports, and even if the mechanism additionally has access to unforgeable auxiliary
information attached to each bid (e.g., trusted timestamps on transactions determined
by a decentralized sequencer [dec, KZGJ20, KDK22, KDL+ 23, Kur20, ZSC+ 20]).

                                          4
1.1    Our Results and Contributions
In this paper we identify two natural relaxations of the dream specification under
which feasibility is restored, and we present an AMM adaptive clinching mechanism
that attains it. Both relaxations are needed: neither one on its own restores feasibility,
as we explain later in this section, after stating our main feasibility result.
 1. No aggregate budget overreporting. Our first relaxation restricts the strategy space.
    A trader may still split itself into arbitrarily many pseudonymous identities and
    report arbitrary values, but the budgets that its identities declare in its own trading
    direction must sum to at most its true budget. No restriction whatsoever is placed
    on the budgets that it declares in the opposite direction: a trader who genuinely
    wishes to sell Y may inject sell-X identities carrying arbitrary finite budgets.
    This assumption is well motivated, because a declared budget must be backed by
    escrowed funds, and that escrow cannot be replenished out of a sibling identity’s
    proceeds during the batch. A trader whose identities collectively declare more
    than it owns would therefore have to fund the excess up front, which by definition
    it cannot do. In practice a trader is of course escrow-limited on the opposite side
    as well, but our guarantees do not need this: they hold even against a deviator
    that can escrow arbitrarily large opposite-direction budgets.
 2. Weak Pareto optimality. Our second relaxation weakens the efficiency requirement
    to weak Pareto optimality for eligible bids, a notion suggested by Li et al. [LSZ26]
    and Shi et al. [SZCL26] for overcoming impossibilities pertaining to full Pareto
    optimality. A bid is eligible if it is willing to trade at the pool’s initial spot price.
    We require that, under truthful reporting, (a) no subset of eligible traders can
    re-trade among themselves, subject to their post-batch holdings, in a way that
    Pareto improves their joint utilities; and (b) no eligible trader can strictly improve
    its utility by making an additional trade with the pool in the direction of its own
    type—a trader who came to sell Y may only sell more Y .
    Intuitively, the impossibility results of Li et al. [LSZ26] and Shi et al. [SZCL26]
    suggest that incentive compatibility is easier to achieve if the mechanism can raise
    the price as competition for the pool’s output intensifies, and close the batch at
    a marginal price exceeding the values of the traders it has served — provided
    that the average price each trader actually pays remains below its value. Weak
    Pareto optimality opens up our design space in this direction, by not counting
    such reverse-direction trades with the pool as Pareto improvements.

Our main result. Under these two relaxations, we obtain the following.
Theorem 1.1 (Sybil-robust and weakly Pareto optimal AMM). Assume that a strate-
gic trader cannot overreport its budget in aggregate across the Sybil identities it submits
in its own trading direction. Then there exists an AMM mechanism that is individu-
ally rational, exactly settles against the pool, and simultaneously satisfies Sybil-robust
incentive compatibility and weak Pareto optimality for eligible bids.
   As argued below, both relaxations are necessary to obtain Theorem 1.1, thus the
combination of properties we achieve is essentially best possible. Moreover, as dis-
cussed later in this section, the techniques used to achieve Theorem 1.1 reveal exciting
connections to classical algorithmic game theory.

                                             5
Necessity of both relaxations. Both relaxations are necessary. If one insists on
full Pareto optimality, impossibility persists even when aggregate same-direction bud-
get overreporting is prohibited [SZCL26].1 Conversely, we prove that if aggregate
same-direction budget overreporting is permitted, impossibility persists even after re-
laxing full Pareto optimality to our weak notion, as stated in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2 (Impossibility when overreporting budget is permitted). Suppose that
traders can arbitrarily misreport their values and budgets. Then, no deterministic
AMM mechanism can simultaneously satisfy Sybil-robust incentive compatibility and
weak Pareto optimality.

    In fact, this impossibility holds in a very strong sense. Its proof needs only de-
viations that replace a single sell-Y report with another, and does not rely on Sybil
identities or opposite-direction orders. Moreover, this impossibility holds even when
the mechanism has access to unforgeable auxiliary information attached to each bid.
    Zhang et al. [ZXS+ 26] prove a similar impossibility. In comparison with our Theo-
rem 1.2, their impossibility holds for a much weaker efficiency notion, but it critically
relies on the builder’s capabilities to reorder bids within a batch, and it ceases to hold
if the underlying consensus can provide trusted timestamps on the submitted bids,
e.g., generated by a decentralized sequencer [dec, KZGJ20, KDK22, KDL+ 23, Kur20,
ZSC+ 20] — indeed, a feasibility result [CWS25] is known in this setting satisfying
their weaker efficiency notion.

Connections to classical algorithmic game theory. To obtain Theorem 1.1, we
generalize the adaptive clinching auction of Dobzinski, Lavi, and Nisan [DLN08], the
classical mechanism for multi-unit auctions with hard budget limits. In the classical
setting, a fixed divisible supply is offered to budget-constrained bidders by an ascending
price clock, and a bidder clinches a unit precisely when the residual demand of the
other bidders no longer covers the remaining supply.
    The challenge in our setting is that an AMM has no fixed, exogenous supply: its
pricing curve makes the supply available for trade depend on the mechanism’s current
state. Our central observation is that adaptive clinching extends to this setting by
replacing fixed supply with a carefully chosen state-dependent target.
    Our work reveals exciting connections between classical algorithmic game theory
(AGT) and modern blockchain mechanism design. On one hand, our results give a
compelling demonstration of how classical AGT ideas contribute to the formation
of a much needed scientific foundation for incentive-compatible blockchain markets.
On the other hand, the proof techniques developed for our AMM setting advance
the state-of-the-art understanding of classical AGT — in particular, we show that the
fixed-supply adaptive clinching auction of Dobzinski, Lavi, and Nisan [DLN08] satisfies
Sybil proofness, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3 (Sybil-proofness of fixed-supply adaptive clinching). In the adaptive
clinching auction for a fixed divisible supply, truthful participation as a single identity

strategy that replaces the bidder with finitely many identities having arbitrary reported

1.2    Additional Related Work
AMM mechanism design. Our study builds directly on the AMM mechanism-
design framework introduced by Chan et al. [CWS25]. They also construct a Sybil-
robust incentive-compatible mechanism, but their result has two limitations. First,
their mechanism does not achieve weak Pareto optimality (wPO). It instead satisfies
weak local efficiency (wLE) for eligible users, which corresponds to only the pool-
trade clause of our wPO definition. Theorem 1.2 shows that this loss of efficiency is
unavoidable in their strategy space, which permits a trader to overreport its aggregate
budget. Second, they require an overly stringent assumption, that the consensus
layer assigns a trustworthy arrival time to every bid. Although a line of work [dec,
KZGJ20, KDK22, KDL+ 23, Kur20, ZSC+ 20] has explored how to provide such a
trusted timestamp, existing schemes are known to be manipulatable, e.g., by a client
who deliberately creates Condorcet cycles [VK23].
    Subsequent work [LSZ26, SZCL26] further delineates the feasibility frontier in this
model. Li et al. [LSZ26] prove a trilemma among incentive compatibility, weak local
efficiency, and uniform pricing: any two of these properties can be achieved, but no
mechanism can satisfy all three. Thus, contrary to a common intuition, clearing a
batch at a uniform price does not by itself yield strong MEV resilience while preserving
even weak local efficiency.
    Li et al. [LSZ26] and Shi et al. [SZCL26] also show that full Pareto optimality is fun-
damentally incompatible with Sybil-robust incentive compatibility. Shi et al. [SZCL26]

                                            7
circumvent this barrier by relaxing exact settlement to permit “burning”. Their model
allows the pool to release more units than are allocated to traders, with the surplus
diverted for the benefit of the broader community. For example, the surplus can subsi-
dize transaction fees, reward liquidity providers, or be returned to the pool to reduce
the price impact of future trades. Under this relaxation, they construct a simple and
computationally efficient “dream” AMM that achieves both full Pareto optimality and
Sybil-robust incentive compatibility.

Sybil proofness in mechanism design. Sybil-proofness, also known as false-name-
proofness, has been studied in a variety of settings, including voting [Con08, ABEP11],
resource exchange [CDLY22], and combinatorial auctions [SYM99, YSM01, YSM04,
ICO+ 10]. A recurring theme is that auctions that are truthful when identities can-
not be faked may cease to be truthful once Sybil attacks are possible; most notably,
the VCG mechanism is not false-name-proof in general [SYM99, YSM04, GLT20]. To
the best of our knowledge, the Sybil resilience of adaptive-clinching-style auctions for
divisible goods under budget constraints has not been analyzed before. The closest
related works are those of Iwasaki et al. [IYT03] and Sakurai et al. [SSIY08]. Both
works aim to design Sybil-resilient auctions for indivisible goods. Iwasaki et al. [IYT03]
focuses ona setting without budget constraints, and Sakurai et al. [SSIY08] considers
non-quasi-linear utilities which include budget constraints. Since their settings and
auction mechanics are fundamentally different from ours, their Sybil-proofness argu-
ments do not carry over to our setting. Moreover, both Iwasaki et al. [IYT03] and
Sakurai et al. [SSIY08] sacrifice efficiency to obtain Sybil resilience. In fact, in the
setting of Iwasaki et al., Pareto efficiency is known to be incompatible with Sybil-
proofness [YSM04]. Thus Iwasaki et al. evaluate efficiency only empirically, through
simulations, and Sakurai et al. leave the analysis of efficiency loss to future work. In
contrast, we identify meaningful relaxations under which we provide provable efficiency
guarantees.

Adaptive clinching and extensions. The original work by Dobzinsi et al. [DLN08]
that proposed the adaptive clinching auction assumed that the budgets are public (i.e.,
unforgeable). The subsequent work of Bhattacharya et al. [BCMX10] show that, for
a divisible good, underreporting a budget is never profitable, so adaptive clinching
is incentive compatible as long as budgets cannot be overreported. It is also known
that with private budgets, simultaneously achieving incentive compatibility and Pareto
optimality is impossible [DLN08]. Our feasibility and infeasibility pair (Theorem 1.1
and Theorem 1.2) can thus be viewed as AMM analogue of classical budget-constrained
auctions of divisible goods.
    Adaptive clinching [DLN08] has been extended to polymatroidal environments,
including the AdWords polytope for sponsored search with multiple keywords and
slots [GMP12], as well as to other sponsored-search and combinatorial settings [CBHLS12,
FLSS11], heterogeneous items [DHS12], two-sided markets [HS22], online supply [GMP13],
and payment constraints more general than hard budgets [GMP14]. Most of these
works assume public budgets, and none considers Sybil identities. More fundamen-
tally, in all of them the feasible allocations are fixed exogenously or, with online sup-
ply, revealed independently of the bids. In an AMM, the supply is instead created by
the very payments the auction collects, along the pool’s cost curve. Our target-based

                                            8
framework captures this endogenous supply, and it recovers fixed and online supply
as constant targets (Section A). Our two-sided setting also differs from that of Hirai
and Sato [HS22], whose sellers are assumed to be truthful and are paid at least their
reserve prices. In contrast, our pool must receive exactly the amount that returns it
to its curve, and traders in both directions are strategic.
    Even with public budgets, no deterministic mechanism attains the guarantees of
adaptive clinching once identical items may be complements [LM12], marginal values
decrease [GMP12, CBHLS12], or items are heterogeneous with multi-dimensional valu-
ations [DHS12]. Another line of work replaces Pareto optimality with approximations
of liquid welfare: Dobzinski and Paes Leme [DP14] show that adaptive clinching 2-
approximates
        √      it when budgets are public, and Lu and Xiao [LX15] improve the ratio
to (1+ 5)/2 with a different auction. Devanur et al. [DHH13] use clinching as a build-
ing block for prior-free revenue and welfare guarantees when agents share a common
budget. We instead retain an exact, albeit weaker, Pareto guarantee.

Other related work. Ferreira and Parkes [FP23] suggest having the consensus layer
enforce verifiable, application-specific sequencing rules that limit how a block builder
may order transactions, and they give such rules for AMMs with weak but provable
guarantees against MEV extraction. Their approach has several limitations. First, it
places application-specific logic in the consensus layer, a shared infrastructure layer
that ought to remain application agnostic. Second, their framework is overly restrictive
and requires transactions to be executed one by one in the order in which they appear
in the block, thus leading to strong impossibilities. In particular, risk-free profit cannot
be eliminated in their model.
    Among other work on AMMs, Milionis, Moallemi, and Roughgarden [MMR24]
characterize profit-maximizing demand curves for a market maker among those that
elicit truthful reports from traders. Since traders in their model trade directly with the
market maker, strategic ordering and attacks such as front-running and back-running
fall outside its scope. Bartoletti et al. [BCLL22] compute a block builder’s optimal
MEV-extraction strategy — their goal is to characterize MEV extraction rather than
to prevent it.
    Batch trading has also been studied as an approach to mitigating MEV. Canidio
and Fritsch [CF23, CF24] propose a function-maximizing AMM and analyze the elim-
ination of arbitrage profits under idealized perfect competition among arbitrageurs
sharing a common external reference price. CoW Protocol [cow] deploys batch trad-
ing through competition among solvers. Canidio and Henneke [CH24] propose fair
combinatorial auctions for trade intents, studying the trade-off between execution fair-
ness and total value returned to traders under strategic solver bidding. Ramseyer
et al. [RGGM24] study equilibrium computation and design trade-offs for batch ex-
changes incorporating CFMMs. These works do not establish incentive compatibil-
ity for finite populations of strategic traders, which is our focus. Within the AMM
mechanism-design framework discussed above, Li et al. [LSZ26] show that uniform pric-
ing cannot be combined with both incentive compatibility and weak local efficiency.
    In a smart-contract implementation, the escrow that prevents budget overreporting
must be protected against flash loans [QZLG21], which would let a trader borrow funds
and repay the loan within the same transaction. The attack can be avoided by splitting
execution into two phases: traders escrow their budgets in one block, and the batch is

                                             9
settled in a later block, so that no flash loan can span both.

continuous evolution of Section 3.3 until the next event. This continuation rule applies
for every S ≥ 0. In particular, clearing the current chord does not by itself terminate

continue to govern the auction while the target is reevaluated as the clock rises.

preempt either operation.

At every finite clock price and finite auction state, the chord target satisfies T amm < x0 ,
and hence the cumulative allocation satisfies X ≤ T amm < x0 . Thus neither the target
nor the allocation reaches the capacity boundary at a finite state. The equality S = 0
only says that the current chord has been cleared; if an active identity remains, raising
the clock eventually produces a target strictly above X. Exact settlement, individual
rationality, and the incentive properties of this outcome are proved in Section 4.
    Observe that the rules above refer to the AMM only through the target T amm , its
evolution as the clock rises, and the capacity x0 ; the cost curve C and its marginal ask
a appear only to explain what those rules mean for the AMM. Section 4 exploits this
by proving the guarantees for a general target, and then verifying that T amm qualifies.


4     Proofs for the One-Sided AMM Clinching
      Mechanism
We now prove that the AMM adaptive clinching mechanism satisfies Sybil-robust incen-
tive compatibility as well as weak Pareto optimality for eligible bids. Before we begin
our proof, we first define an abstract target-based clinching mechanism in Section 4.1.
This more generalized abstract mechanism allows us to isolate the proof arguments
from the particular choice of supply function. Specifically, later in Section A, we point
out that the classical ordinary adaptive clinching auction [DLN08, BCMX10], and its
version with exogenous online supply [GMP13], are the special cases in which the tar-
get is constant. Thus, our proofs directly imply that these classical adaptive clinching
auctions are sybil-proof too.
    After defining the abstract target-based mechanism, we derive the explicit continu-
ous rates implied by its primitive conditions. We then verify the abstract requirements
directly for the AMM chord target, and establish the structural lemmas for a single
run. We next compare a merged run with a Sybil run to prove the desired incentive
compatibility properties.

4.1    Abstract Target-Based Clinching Mechanism
The AMM clinching mechanism is driven by its chord target T amm (p, X, Y ), which
determines how much cumulative supply is available at each auction state. We now
abstract this role from the geometry of the AMM. The resulting target-based mech-
anism uses the same clinching rules, but allows a general admissible target function.
Choosing T amm as its target recovers the AMM mechanism.

Convention at capacity saturation. Earlier in our AMM clinching mechanism,
we never saturate the capacity, that is, T amm < x0 at every finite state. However, our
generalized target-based mechanism may reach capacity saturation at a finite state, so
we must explicitly specify what happens afterward. We adopt the following completion
convention: attaining capacity does not itself terminate the execution. The clock and
ordinary valuation exits continue under the usual rules. Theorem 4.5 shows that
this continuation involves no further trade, so we call it the zero-trade completion
convention. Allocations and payments are therefore already economically final; the
continuation only retains effective budgets until their ordinary valuation exits. This
prevents unused budgets from being treated as exhausted merely because the available
capacity has been fully allocated.

7     Impossibility with Budget Overreporting
The positive incentive results above restrict a trader’s aggregate declared budget in
its true direction to its actual budget. We now show that this restriction is essential.
Suppose instead that a trader may declare a larger budget, with the deviation regarded
as feasible whenever the amount actually paid does not exceed its true budget. Even
without Sybil identities or opposite-direction orders, this enlarged strategy space is
incompatible with the other desired properties.
    Under the enlarged strategy space considered here, a true eligible type (V, Q) may
replace its report by any single finite eligible report (v, q). If that report produces
(x, y), the deviation has finite utility precisely when y ≤ Q. Incentive compatibility
with budget overreporting therefore requires

                         y≤Q      =⇒     V xtr − y tr ≥ V x − y,

where (xtr , y tr ) is the truthful outcome. In particular, when the mechanism does not
require advance escrow of the entire declared budget, budget overreporting becomes
possible as long as its realized payment remains affordable.

Theorem 7.1 (Budget-overreporting impossibility). For a constant-product AMM,
there is no deterministic mechanism that, on every profile of two eligible sell-Y users,
simultaneously satisfies:
 1. individual rationality (Theorem 2.3);
 2. exact settlement (Theorem 2.2);
 3. both clauses of weak Pareto optimality in Theorem 2.7; and
 4. incentive compatibility against single-identity eligible value and budget misreports,
    including budget overreports whose realized payment does not exceed the true bud-
    get.
All true types and reports used in the proof are eligible and have finite values and
budgets.

    We stress that this impossibility holds in a strong sense. It assumes neither sym-
metry nor resistance to Sybil or opposite-direction deviations, and it already applies
to the much smaller class of deviations that replace one sell-Y report by another. As

A      Fixed and Online Supply as Constant Targets
The target-based clinching mechanism of Section 4.1 separates the clinching mechanics
from the source of supply. This appendix shows that the two classical supply models
are instances of it: the ordinary adaptive clinching auction [DLN08, BCMX10] for a
fixed divisible supply, and adaptive clinching with exogenous online supply, due to
Goel, Mirrokni, and Paes Leme [GMP13]. In both cases the target ignores the auction
state (X, Y ) and the clock price: it is the constant function equal to the available
supply. The admissibility requirements of Theorem 4.2 then hold trivially.
    The main content of the appendix is a rule-by-rule correspondence showing that,
with a constant target, the rules of Section 3 reduce to those of the classical auction.
The only genuine boundary convention is that the abstract clock starts at a positive
price p⋆ , whereas the classical clock starts at zero. A constant target also attains its
capacity, but the zero-trade completion convention makes that boundary identical to
the classical clock after all supply has been allocated and creates no exception in the
incentive theorem.

A.2     Exogenous Online Supply
In the online supply model of [GMP13, Definition 3.1 and Example 3.2], the supply
of the good is revealed over steps t = 0, 1, . . . , m as a nondecreasing sequence of
cumulative supplies
                              0 = s0 ≤ s1 ≤ · · · ≤ sm .                         (85)
The sequence is exogenous: it may be deterministic, random, or adversarial, but it
does not depend on the reports, and at step t the auctioneer knows only s0 , . . . , st .
The identities, their reports, and their budgets are fixed before the first step. At every
step the auctioneer must announce a cumulative allocation and a cumulative payment
for each identity, and it may only augment those of the previous step. Online adaptive
clinching announces, at step t, the outcome (x(st ), y(st )) of the adaptive clinching
auction of Section A.1 run to completion with supply st . Between steps t − 1 and t it
therefore allocates x(st ) − x(st−1 ) and charges y(st ) − y(st−1 ).
    Let the environment description be the supply revealed so far, and let the target
be its last entry:

Remark A.6 (Two time axes). The online step t and the clock price p are different
objects. At step t the clock is run afresh from p⋆ against the constant target st ; the
auction does not pause one ascending clock and resume it when more supply arrives.
Accordingly, online adaptive clinching is a family of target-based mechanisms indexed
by σt , one for every prefix of the supply path, and not a single run with a growing
target. A statement about one prefix, such as a single-run lemma of Section 4.4 or a
comparison between two bid profiles that face the same σt , is a statement about one
admissible abstract target-based mechanism. Whether the increments x(st ) − x(st−1 )
and y(st ) − y(st−1 ) are nonnegative, so that the family can be implemented online, is
a different kind of statement: it compares the targets Tσt−1 and Tσt on one fixed bid

used nor implied by the results of Section 4. On a profile that has at least two identities
of positive value and budget and no tied values, and with p⋆ below the threshold (84)
for the largest supply sm , which is the smallest threshold along the path, every run is
exactly the auction of [GMP13], and that theorem applies verbatim.

The same conclusions hold for the classical zero-start auctions, with the simultaneous
proportional rule at tied stopping exits.
